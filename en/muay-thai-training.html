Muay Thai for beginners: the art of eight limbs

By Guides · Stephan Koenig · Updated 14 September 2026

Muay Thai is called the art of eight limbs: two fists, two elbows, two knees, two shins. That makes it the most versatile striking art — and the most overwhelming one for beginners. Here is the order that actually makes sense.

What defines Muay Thai

The difference from kickboxing is not just the extra weapons, it is the clinch: the close range where neck control, knees and elbows decide things. Treat Muay Thai as kickboxing with elbows and you miss half of it.

The second difference is the stance. The Muay Thai stance is more upright and square than in boxing — because you have to see and catch kicks, not only punches. It feels open and exposed at first, but it is the foundation for everything else.

The basic techniques

Teep (push kick)

The straight kick with the sole of the foot — the jab of Muay Thai. It holds range, breaks your opponent's rhythm and takes their balance. Few techniques are so underrated by beginners and so constantly used by advanced fighters.

Roundhouse kick

Kicked with the shin, driven by a full body rotation: the standing foot pivots, the hip turns through, and the arm on the kicking side swings down and back as a counterweight. The Muay Thai roundhouse is not a snap like in karate — it swings through.

Elbow

The most dangerous close-range weapon: horizontal, diagonal downward or upward. Use it carefully on the bag — elbows load the joint and do not forgive bad technique.

Knee

Straight forward from the hip, or diagonally out of the clinch. The knee is what decides clinch exchanges, and it doubles as excellent conditioning work.

Clinch

Neck control, breaking balance, landing knees. Without a partner the clinch can only be trained partially — but on the bag you can practise the grip, the pull and the knee by actively steering the bag instead of just hitting it.

Common Muay Thai mistakes

  • Snapping the roundhouse instead of swinging through — the power comes from rotation, not from the knee.
  • Neglecting the teep — without it you have no tool to create distance.
  • Keeping the standing leg stiff — it has to pivot, otherwise your own body brakes the kick.
  • Elbows too early and too hard — practise them with control on the bag or the joint pays.
  • Skipping the clinch — it decides fights and is almost always left out of beginner training.

Frequently asked questions

Is Muay Thai suitable for beginners?

Yes. The entry point is a handful of techniques — teep, roundhouse to the body, jab and cross. Elbows, knees and clinch come later. Start structured and it never gets overwhelming.

How does Muay Thai differ from kickboxing?

Muay Thai allows elbows and extensive clinch work with knees; kickboxing does not, or only in limited form. The stance is also more upright, and kicks land with the shin rather than the instep.

Can you train Muay Thai at home?

Technique, conditioning and bag work yes. The clinch needs a partner — that is the part home training cannot replace. For everything else a bag, a timer and a plan are enough.

Does shin conditioning hurt?

At the start yes, if you overdo it. Shins adapt over months. Increase gradually, kick cleanly with the shin rather than the instep, and take rest days — conditioning is not the same as injuring yourself.
